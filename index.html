ProofBench: A Lean-based Formal Benchmark for AI Capabilities in Combinatorics

Vaibhav Bajpai1, Nathan Bowler3, Johannes Carmesin1, Jinghua Groppe2, Sven Groppe2, and Utku Okur1

  1. Institute of Discrete Mathematics and Algebra, Faculty of Mathematics and Computer Science, Technische Universität Bergakademie Freiberg
  2. Professorship for Artificial Intelligence, Faculty of Mathematics and Computer Science, Technische Universität Bergakademie Freiberg
  3. Department of Mathematics, Universität Hamburg

October 2026

Abstract

We survey benchmarks to test Artificial Intelligence (AI) capabilities in mathematics. Our survey classifies benchmarks along the axes informal/formal and whether they accept a single answer or a proof. Next, we introduce ProofBench, a benchmark initiative aimed at measuring the ability of artificial intelligence systems to perform research-level mathematical reasoning. ProofBench consists of 114 problems, most of them in combinatorics and 82 in graph theory. It is designed as a formal-to-formal benchmark suite in Lean 4, requiring systems to produce mechanically checkable proofs of research-level mathematical statements. We position ProofBench within the landscape of existing AI mathematics benchmarks, describe our construction pipeline, introduce the theorem sandwich methodology for validating formalized statements, and report on an inaugural proof-of-concept workshop.

We specify the protocol by which AI systems are evaluated on ProofBench: agents work in a sandboxed harness with a uniform set of tools, over a bounded number of fresh sessions linked by structured handoff notes, and a submission counts as a solution only if it compiles in Lean 4 against the unchanged formal statement. For every attempt we record cost, tokens, time and proof length. Our evaluation harness is built on Inspect AI and designed to work on both a local PC and a high-performance computing (HPC) cluster.

1The Problem Set

ProofBench currently contains 114 problems (Table 1). Each problem is one Lean 4 file containing the definitions it needs and a single main theorem whose proof is replaced by sorry; the task is to replace it with a complete proof. The project is pinned to Lean 4.33.1 and the corresponding Mathlib release.

Areas. By Mathematics Subject Classification, 82 problems are in graph theory (05C), 8 in designs, configurations and matroids (05B), and 5 in extremal set theory (05D); the remaining 19 range over number theory, discrete and convex geometry, group theory, probability, order theory, set theory and theoretical computer science.

Two libraries. Definitions that are needed to state problems but are missing from Mathlib live in a statement library of 47 Lean modules, which the evaluated systems can see and import. Everything that would help to solve problems (the theorem sandwiches, reference proofs and their auxiliary results, 89 modules in total) lives in a separate proctor library that never enters the evaluation environment.

Open problems. For each of the 32 open problems we also provide its negation as a second Lean statement. A system answers an open problem by proving either the statement or its negation, so that the formulation does not reveal which side we expect to hold.

Reference solutions. Eighteen problems, among them ten of the fourteen calibration problems, have complete Lean reference proofs. They test the grading pipeline end to end and give a lower bound on the length of a formal proof.

    2Benchmarking AI in Mathematics

    A formal benchmark requires the AI system to produce output in a formal proof language, such as Lean 4, that can be mechanically checked for correctness; an informal benchmark accepts natural-language output, which must then be evaluated by human judges or an LLM-as-a-judge. A proof-based benchmark requires the model to construct a full mathematical argument, whereas a final-answer benchmark only demands a specific piece of data as the solution.     The survey shows that competition-level formal theorem proving is beginning to approach saturation, that undergraduate and graduate-level formal reasoning remains extremely challenging for current AI models, and that at the research level the landscape of benchmarks narrows considerably. ProofBench is a broadly scoped formal and proof-based benchmark at the research level, with a present emphasis on combinatorics and graph theory.

    2.1Properties of an ideal benchmark

    1. Research-level mathematical depth. The benchmark should contain problems whose difficulty and significance are recognised by active research mathematicians; problems should be selected by domain experts.
    2. Disciplinary breadth. A benchmark covering diverse areas of mathematics, including fields under-represented in the current formalization ecosystem, is better positioned to assess genuine reasoning skills.
    3. Reproducible and objective evaluation. Unambiguous criteria for measuring mathematical performance, so that results are reproducible and comparable across AI systems and experimental settings.
    4. Independence. The benchmark should be designed and governed by researchers free of conflicts of interest regarding the systems under evaluation, with transparent funding and no commercial stake in the outcome.

    3The Theorem Sandwich

    Formalizing a theorem statement carries a significant risk that the resulting Lean 4 code does not fully match the author’s intended meaning. To reduce this risk, we use a theorem sandwich. Alongside the theorem statement, we formalize two kinds of additional material. First, the upper bound: a layer of key lemmata, or a stronger known or conjectured statement, which together imply the theorem, along with a Lean proof of the implication. Second, the lower bound: a handful of easily derivable corollaries, along with their derivations (Figure 3).

    A statement that is accidentally too strong makes the upper bound unprovable; one that is accidentally too weak fails to yield the corollaries. Lemmata of the upper bound may remain unproved (sorry): if every one of them were proved, the problem would be solved. Of the 114 problems, 87 currently have both bounds.

    4Evaluation Protocol

    4.1Harness and sandbox

    We use the open-source evaluation framework Inspect AI of the UK AI Security Institute. Each problem is attempted in its own container, built from an image that contains the Lean toolchain, Mathlib, and the statement library of the selected problems only. The container has no network access (except in runs with web search), no host environment and in particular no API keys, and no access to other problems, to the grading material or to the evaluation logs. On a workstation the containers run under Docker; on HPC clusters they run under Apptainer through our sandbox package inspect-apptainer. Tools available to the agent are:

    • a shell (bash) inside the sandbox with the Lean toolchain, Mathlib and the ProofBench statement library;
    • the Lean language server through lean-lsp-mcp (goal states, diagnostics, Mathlib search);
    • web search, in a separate configuration, through one external search provider for all models;
    • a file-editing tool, a Python interpreter with sympy and networkx, SageMath (with GAP and nauty), and the SAT solver CaDiCaL.

    4.2Sessions, limits and handoff

    Each model attempts each problem in at most 10 sessions. A session is a fresh context: the agent starts from the current state of its files, the Lean compiler output for them, and a handoff note written in the previous session in a fixed format listing proved lemmata, open goals, approaches that failed, and any Lean code drafted but not yet saved. After every session the independent Lean check of Section 4.3 is applied; the first session that passes ends the chain (Figure 4).

    4.3Grading

    A submission is graded in a fresh container that receives only the source text of the submitted files, in two layers, following the designs of LeanEval and LeanOpenProblems.

    Layer 1: static check and compilation. The theorem statement and every definition of the challenge file must be unchanged. Imports are restricted to Mathlib, the ProofBench statement library and the agent’s own helper files. No file may contain sorry, new axioms, native_decide, code that runs at elaboration time, or options that skip kernel checks. The files must compile, and the main theorem may depend only on the axioms propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound.

    Layer 2: Comparator. If the first layer passes, Comparator, the Lean FRO’s adversarial proof checker, builds the trusted statement and the submission separately in a Landlock sandbox, requires their statements and the definitions they depend on to be identical, replays the proof through the Lean kernel, and checks the axioms. A submission counts as a solution only if both layers accept it. Solutions of open problems are in addition checked by hand before any claim is made.

    4.4Measured quantities

    For every attempt we record the verdict, the number of sessions used until success, input and output tokens, costs, total time and working time, the number of tool and model calls, and the proof length. Per run we freeze the harness commit, the sandbox image, the Lean toolchain and Mathlib commit, the ProofBench commit of the problem set, the model identifier, its reasoning-effort setting, and whether web search was enabled.

    6The Inaugural Workshop

    To validate the construction pipeline, we organized the inaugural ProofBench workshop at TU Bergakademie Freiberg from 22 September to 3 October 2025. A team of approximately twenty participants, research mathematicians with combinatorics backgrounds and a smaller cohort of Lean experts, worked together on the formalization of a recent result in combinatorics: a counterexample to Andreae’s ubiquity conjecture, due to Carmesin. The exercise confirmed that research-level formalization presents difficulties even for those with substantial experience, and it validated the theorem sandwich (Listing 1).

    7Contributing Problems

    Problems enter ProofBench as follows:

    1. A researcher or research group proposes a result, a conjecture, or a question from their field.
    2. Our team formalizes the statement in Lean 4, either together with the researcher or independently, and builds its theorem sandwich.
    3. The statement, together with the definitions it needs, enters the benchmark; the sandwich and any reference proof are kept in a separate library that the evaluated systems never see.
