Why now

On 6 October 2026, OpenAI released several hundred manuscripts written by an internal model, many with Lean proofs checked by Comparator. Four of them resolve conjectures that were open problems in ProofBench at the time:

  • refutedHadwiger’s conjecture
  • refutedSidorenko’s conjecture (bipartite graphs with bounded sides)
  • refutedRyser’s hypergraph conjecture
  • provedSeymour’s second neighbourhood conjecture

Comparator-checked Lean proofs, not yet independently verified.

AI in research mathematics is no longer hypothetical. It now needs to be measured reliably, by people with no stake in the outcome.

§1 · The problem set

114 problems. Each one is a single sorry.

Every problem is one Lean 4 file with the definitions it needs and one main theorem whose proof is replaced by sorry. The task is to replace it with a complete proof. Open problems are posed in prove-or-disprove form: we also ship the negation, so the formulation does not reveal which side we expect to hold.

    §2 · Where ProofBench sits

    Proofs, not answers. Lean, not judges.

    Benchmarks for AI mathematics differ along two axes: whether the output is formal (checked by a proof assistant) or informal, and whether it is a full proof or a final answer. Competition-level formal proving is close to saturation. At the research level, the field thins out.

    What an ideal benchmark needs

    1. i

      Research-level depth

      Problems whose difficulty and significance active research mathematicians recognise, selected by domain experts.

    2. ii

      Disciplinary breadth

      Fields under-represented in the formalization ecosystem. We begin with combinatorics and graph theory.

    3. iii

      Reproducible, objective evaluation

      Unambiguous criteria: a proof is correct if and only if Lean says so, the same way for every system.

    4. iv

      Independence

      Designed and governed by researchers with no commercial stake in the systems under evaluation.

    §3 · The theorem sandwich

    How do you know the Lean statement says what the mathematician meant?

    A formal statement can silently miss its author’s intended meaning, a recurring problem for formal benchmarks. We pin each statement between two layers of independent evidence. 87 of the 114 problems currently have both bounds.

    What if the formalization is…

    Upper bound

    Key lemmata, or a stronger known or conjectured statement

    Some lemmata may stay sorry: proving all of them would solve the problem.

    ⟹

    The statement

    theorem Pxxx : … := by sorry

    The formal statement the AI system must prove.

    ⟹

    Lower bound

    A handful of easily derivable corollaries

    Each derived from the statement in Lean.

    §4 · Evaluation

    Same sandbox, same tools, same budget, for every model.

    Systems run in a uniform harness built on Inspect AI, not in each vendor’s own agent product, so that results are comparable and reproducible.

    1

    A sealed sandbox

    One container per problem holding the Lean toolchain, Mathlib and the statement library. It has no network, no API keys, and no grading material. Docker on workstations, Apptainer on HPC clusters.

    2

    Real research tools

    • bash with the Lean toolchain
    • Lean language server via lean-lsp-mcp
    • Python with sympy and networkx
    • SageMath (GAP, nauty) and the SAT solver CaDiCaL
    • optional web search through a single shared provider

    3

    Up to ten fresh sessions

    Each session starts from a clean context with the current files, the compiler output and a fixed-format handoff note listing proved lemmas, open goals and failed approaches.

    Anatomy of an attempt

    illustration · not a real result

      Layer 1 · static check & compilation

      • theorem statement and every definition unchanged
      • imports restricted to Mathlib, the statement library and the agent’s own files
      • no sorry, new axioms, native_decide or kernel-skipping options
      • compiles, depending only on propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound

      Layer 2 · Comparator

      • the Lean FRO’s adversarial checker, run in a Landlock sandbox
      • builds the trusted statement and the submission separately
      • requires the statements and their definitions to be identical
      • replays the proof through the Lean kernel and checks the axioms

      Recorded for every attempt

      • verdict
      • sessions used
      • input & output tokens
      • cost
      • total & working time
      • tool & model calls
      • proof length

      Per run we freeze the harness commit, sandbox image, Lean & Mathlib commit, problem-set commit, model identifier, reasoning effort and search setting. Solutions to open problems are also checked by hand before any claim is made.

      §6 · The paper

      ProofBench: A Lean-based Formal Benchmark for AI Capabilities in Combinatorics

      Vaibhav Bajpai1, Nathan Bowler3, Johannes Carmesin1, Jinghua Groppe2, Sven Groppe2, Utku Okur1

      1. Institute of Discrete Mathematics and Algebra, TU Bergakademie Freiberg
      2. Professorship for Artificial Intelligence, TU Bergakademie Freiberg
      3. Department of Mathematics, Universität Hamburg
      Abstract

      We survey benchmarks to test Artificial Intelligence (AI) capabilities in mathematics. Our survey classifies benchmarks along the axes informal/formal and whether they accept a single answer or a proof. Next, we introduce ProofBench, a benchmark initiative aimed at measuring the ability of artificial intelligence systems to perform research-level mathematical reasoning. ProofBench consists of 114 problems, most of them in combinatorics and 82 in graph theory. It is designed as a formal-to-formal benchmark suite in Lean 4, requiring systems to produce mechanically checkable proofs of research-level mathematical statements. We position ProofBench within the landscape of existing AI mathematics benchmarks, describe our construction pipeline, introduce the theorem sandwich methodology for validating formalized statements, and report on an inaugural proof-of-concept workshop.

      We specify the protocol by which AI systems are evaluated on ProofBench: agents work in a sandboxed harness with a uniform set of tools, over a bounded number of fresh sessions linked by structured handoff notes, and a submission counts as a solution only if it compiles in Lean 4 against the unchanged formal statement. For every attempt we record cost, tokens, time and proof length. Our evaluation harness is built on Inspect AI and designed to work on both a local PC and a high-performance computing (HPC) cluster.

      §7 · People

      Built by mathematicians, with the Lean community.

      The inaugural ProofBench workshop took place at TU Bergakademie Freiberg from 22 September to 3 October 2025. About twenty research mathematicians with combinatorics backgrounds and a smaller group of Lean experts formalized a recent result together: a counterexample to Andreae’s ubiquity conjecture. The workshop tested the construction pipeline and the theorem sandwich, and it showed how hard research-level formalization is, even for experts.

        You propose

        A result, a conjecture or a question from your field, ideally one whose significance experts recognise.

        We formalize

        Our team states it in Lean 4, with you or independently, and builds its theorem sandwich.

      3. 3

        It enters the benchmark

        The statement and the definitions it needs go public. The sandwich and any reference proof stay in the proctor library.
